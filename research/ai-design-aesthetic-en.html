The Beige Internet

Kyle Chayka, a staff writer at The New Yorker, described the look that AI-built websites keep converging on — and named where it comes from. This page collects what he actually claims and where to read it. It is set in the style under discussion on purpose: every tell on the list is applied to the page itself.

Kyle Chayka The New Yorker · the Kyle Chayka Industries newsletter
The claim

One house style leaked into everything

Chayka is not making the broad point that AI makes ugly things. His point is narrower: one particular model has a persistent default taste, and that taste matches the brand of the company that built it. Because the model is used at scale to build landing pages, decks and dashboards, one vendor’s house style now reads as the house style of the current web.

He has been on this beat for a while — his book Filterworld is about the way algorithmic platforms flatten culture. This piece is the same argument moved from the recommendation feed to interface generation.

The key exhibit

The vendor says so itself

The model has strong design instincts, with a consistent default house style. This default is persistent.

A line from Anthropic’s own guidance, which Chayka cites as confirmation: the set of tells he lists is not a quirk of his sample but a stated property of the tool. He then notes that those tells match Anthropic’s own branding.

The conclusion

The default goes to whoever doesn’t push back

Leave the model unchallenged and you get the generic result — “the lowest common acceptable denominator of aesthetics and taste.” The designers Chayka spoke to think distinctive work with AI is still possible, but that it costs real labour: mood boards, hand sketches, many iterations. The cure is not a different model; it is what you bring to the brief.
